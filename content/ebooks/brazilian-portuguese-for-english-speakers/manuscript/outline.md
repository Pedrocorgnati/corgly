# Brazilian Portuguese for English Speakers (working title)

Working title: "Brazilian Portuguese for English Speakers: A Strategy Guide for False Beginners (working title)". The final English title is still open.

## Reader, variant and notation

- Target reader: a false beginner, an adult self-learner who already had contact with an app or a course and now needs priorities, routines and contrastive explanations.
- Variant: Brazilian Portuguese only, the variant certified by Celpe-Bras [F7]. The exam is cited only as the reference for the variant; this book makes no promise about passing any exam.
- Regional reference: broad Brazilian pronunciation, with a regional note only where the variant changes what the reader hears.
- Notation policy: respelling for English speakers in the body text, with a respelling key in the front matter. Broad IPA in square brackets only in Chapter 3, Chapter 4 and the minimal-pairs list of the appendix. Chapters 1, 2 and 5 use respelling only.
- Audio: out of scope (the catalog delivers one PDF per book). The book offers transcription only; it never promises audio files or QR codes.
- Examples: written from scratch for this audience. Author's own examples are marked as illustrations and are queued for native-speaker review.

## Chapter 1: Why Brazilian Portuguese Is Close and Far

- Objective: set honest expectations about what English transfers well (Latin and French vocabulary) and what it does not (sound contrasts and grammar traps), so the reader knows where to spend time.
- English-speaker difficulty addressed: the false sense of distance or of ease; the reader underestimates pronunciation work and overestimates grammar work.
- Pillar(s): Pillar 1 (cognate engine with false-friend brake) as motivation only; Pillar 3 (intelligibility hierarchy) preview.
- Sources: [F9] [F10] [F7].
- Planned boxes: "If you speak Spanish" box with three traps relevant to this overview plus the full-stop b, d, g drill ("cada", "lado", "fogo") [F62] [F36]. No UK/Australian English box in this chapter.
- Self-test: 10 questions with answer key [F94].
- Spaced review plan: D+1, D+3, D+8 and D+27, presented as the author's suggestion, an adaptation of the interval idea in [F90], which measures a single gap between two study sessions. Not presented as a result of that source.
- Planned content:
  - lexical similarity between Portuguese and English is about 0.20, against about 0.89 with Spanish and about 0.80 with Italian [F9].
  - about 28.30% of English vocabulary comes from French and about 28.24% from Latin, while about 83% of the most frequent English words are native [F10]; what this means for reading versus conversation.
  - what the book covers, what it excludes (no audio, no exam promise, no vocabulary numeric targets).
  - how to read the respelling key and the "changes meaning / accent only" labels.

## Chapter 2: Your English Is a Head Start: Cognates and False Friends

- Objective: convert English vocabulary into Portuguese quickly through regular suffix patterns, then brake where the pattern lies.
- English-speaker difficulty addressed: false cognates cost more than they help; the reader trusts spelling similarity blindly.
- Pillar(s): Pillar 1 (cognate engine with false-friend brake); Pillar 9 (error deck) as the memory frame.
- Sources ([F13] and [F38] are reverse-direction studies of Brazilians learning English): [F11] [F12] [F13] [F14] [F38] [F39] [F40] [F41] [F42] [F43] [F44] [F45] [F46] [F47] [F51] [F53] [F55] [F56].
- Planned boxes: "If you speak Spanish" box with three traps relevant to cognates and false friends plus the full-stop b, d, g drill ("cada", "lado", "fogo") [F62] [F36]. No UK/Australian English box in this chapter.
- Self-test: 10 questions with answer key [F94].
- Spaced review plan: D+1, D+3, D+8 and D+27, the author's suggestion, an adaptation of the interval idea in [F90], which measures a single gap between two study sessions.
- Planned content:
  - at least six regular suffix patterns as tendencies, not rules: ph to f, -ize to -izar, -fy to -ficar, -age to -agem, -tion to -ção, -ous to -oso, -ly to -mente, plus plural -vel to -veis [F11] [F12].
  - the exception the reader must learn up front: "difference" becomes "diferença", not "diferência" [F11].
  - core false-friend list with dictionary verbs: "pretender" [F39], "puxar" [F40], "assistir" [F41], "livraria" [F42], "atualmente" [F43], "eventualmente" [F44], "realizar" [F45], "parentes" [F46], plus "oficina" and "particular" (private) [F12].
  - extended list from the false-cognate survey [F47]; frequency before exoticism, with the vocabulary gain treated as continuous and without any numeric target [F53].
  - why false cognates hurt: slower and less accurate processing in a study of Brazilian learners of English, that is, the reverse direction from this audience [F38].
  - cognate facilitation comes from spelling and meaning similarity, not sound, in a study of Brazilians in first contact with English, again the reverse direction [F13]; cognates and concrete words are learned better and forgotten more slowly [F14].
  - chunks taught as blocks ("Tudo bem?", "Pois é", "Tanto faz", "Dar uma olhada") because formulaic sequences are read faster [F51].
  - daily 10-minute routine from Pillar 1: convert, read aloud, then self-test the meaning in full sentences; incidental exposure alone yields little [F55], and tasks with need, search and evaluation fix more [F56].

## Chapter 3: Three Contrasts English Ears Miss: Aspiration, Mid Vowels, Nasals

- Objective: train perception first on the three contrasts English does not mark, then move to controlled production.
- English-speaker difficulty addressed: aspiration of p, t, k; the e/open-e and o/open-o contrast; nasal vowels and nasal diphthongs.
- Pillar(s): Pillar 2 (the three contrasts English ears do not mark).
- Sources ([F24] is a reverse-direction study of Brazilians learning English): [F18] [F21] [F23] [F24] [F26] [F33] [F34].
- Planned boxes: "If you speak Spanish" box with three traps relevant to these contrasts plus the full-stop b, d, g drill ("cada", "lado", "fogo") [F62] [F36]. "UK/Australian English" box: non-rhotic speakers do not use a flap bridge, so the aspiration bridge must be built another way; vowel notes for UK and Australian English.
- Self-test: 10 questions with answer key [F94].
- Spaced review plan: D+1, D+3, D+8 and D+27, the author's suggestion, an adaptation of the interval idea in [F90], which measures a single gap between two study sessions.
- Planned content:
  - aspiration: voice onset time of about 55, 70 and 80 ms in English against about 12, 18 and 38 ms in Brazilian Portuguese [F24]; limitation stated in the same section: that study follows Brazilians learning English, so the application to Portuguese pairs is an inference. A stop with zero or negative voice onset time is heard as voiced by English speakers [F24], and the two languages use voice onset time differently [F26].
  - mid vowels: English speakers miss both perception and production of the open and closed mid vowels, and advanced learners overcorrect [F18]; the book uses broad IPA here and minimal pairs ("avô" and "avó", "pôde" and "pode", "sede" and "sede") as author's illustrations queued for native review.
  - nasals: Brazilian Portuguese has five nasal vowels, in a description based on a single speaker [F21], and the type of nasal vowel weighs on the accent judgment of English speakers more than hearing a native model beforehand [F23].
  - perception training with minimal pairs in several voices before any production exercise [F34]; the gain in production is smaller and less retained [F33].
  - author's suggestions only, without numbers and without source claims: the paper test in front of the mouth to feel aspiration; the spin/stop bridge for unaspirated p, t, k; a suggested training order starting with "ão" and "em".

## Chapter 4: Sounds That Change Meaning Versus Sounds That Are Only Accent

- Objective: rank pronunciation work by what it buys: meaning contrasts first, accent-only features after, with the declared goal of being understood without effort.
- English-speaker difficulty addressed: the tap and the strong r, s voicing in coda, vocalized l, palatalization, stress and rhythm.
- Pillar(s): Pillar 3 (intelligibility hierarchy) and Pillar 4 (de-Americanizing the consonants).
- Sources: [F17] [F20] [F25] [F27] [F28] [F29] [F30] [F32] [F33] [F34] [F35].
- Planned boxes: "If you speak Spanish" box with three traps relevant to these sounds plus the full-stop b, d, g drill ("cada", "lado", "fogo") [F62] [F36]. "UK/Australian English" box: no flap bridge for non-rhotic speakers, and the coda r in careful speech; vowel notes for UK and Australian English.
- Self-test: 10 questions with answer key [F94].
- Spaced review plan: D+1, D+3, D+8 and D+27, the author's suggestion, an adaptation of the interval idea in [F90], which measures a single gap between two study sessions.
- Planned content:
  - meaning-changing column versus accent-only column: declared goal is intelligibility, and accent, comprehensibility and intelligibility are partially independent [F29] [F30].
  - the tap [ɾ] and the strong r; English speakers reach for approximants, an l-like quality, epenthesis or a trill for the tap [F17], and for approximants, deletion or a schwa diphthong for the strong r [F25]. The deletion rate of 93% in that study refers to the English-speaking learners in the study, not to native speakers [F25], and native r deletion in final position in verbs is normal use [F20].
  - s voicing in coda before voiced sounds: "os dois" and "desde" [F27]; English speakers do not make this assimilation even after more than 10 years in Brazil [F27].
  - vocalized l in coda and palatalization of t and d before [i], including final -e [F20]; stress on the last two syllables and the debated rhythm question [F28].
  - training in three steps per sound (controlled, semi-controlled, free) because pronunciation teaching pays off on specific features and controlled production [F32]; shadowing for listening at lower levels [F35]; perception training again [F34], with the smaller production gain noted [F33].
  - author's suggestions only, without numbers and without source claims: the "better" flap bridge for US English speakers only; a warning against importing English schwa into pretonic vowels; a note that the coda l is listed as accent-only in this book; a marked regional note for the Rio de Janeiro s in coda.

## Chapter 5: The Grammar Traps English Sets

- Objective: fix the four contrasts that English creates (copula, subjunctive future, present perfect, object position) and the noun-level traps (gender, regência).
- English-speaker difficulty addressed: one copula, present for future reference, present perfect transfer, obligatory expressed objects, gender and preposition regência.
- Pillar(s): Pillar 5 (grammatical contrast with English).
- Sources: [F15] [F57] [F58] [F59] [F60] [F61] [F63] [F64] [F65] [F66] [F67] [F68] [F69] [F70] [F71] [F72] [F73].
- Planned boxes: "If you speak Spanish" box with three traps relevant to this grammar plus the full-stop b, d, g drill ("cada", "lado", "fogo") [F62] [F36]. No UK/Australian English box in this chapter.
- Self-test: 10 questions with answer key [F94].
- Spaced review plan: D+1, D+3, D+8 and D+27, the author's suggestion, an adaptation of the interval idea in [F90], which measures a single gap between two study sessions.
- Planned content:
  - "ser" and "estar" without the permanent versus temporary rule, which fails on locative sentences [F57]; probabilistic teaching tested with American students [F58]; overgeneralization of "ser" and omission of "estar" in Spanish L2 cited only as an analogy [F59].
  - subjunctive future where English uses the present [F60]; mood is hard for L2 learners in general [F61].
  - present perfect transferred by native English speakers at all levels [F71], with the author's illustration of "I've seen this movie" versus "Já vi esse filme".
  - null objects and the overgeneralization of omission [F63]; the clitic "o/a" is in disuse in speech and persists in writing, with "ele/ela" or null object in its place, according to the abstract of [F64]; initial proclisis is common in Brazilian speech [F65].
  - subject expression in Brazilian Portuguese moving toward overt pronouns [F15]; "a gente" replacing "nós" [F67] and competing with it in complement functions [F66]; regional "tu" with third-person verb [F68].
  - gender errors mix transfer and overgeneralization with English L1 among the groups [F69]; the error is lexical assignment, seen in Spanish L2, not a syntactic deficit [F70]; learn nouns with their article.
  - existential "ter" over "haver" in speech [F72]; regência as interference between English and Portuguese, with the limitation that the cited literature mostly covers Portuguese speakers learning English [F73].
  - author's suggestions only, without numbers and without source claims: preposition plus article contractions, the possessive article, double negation as the norm, and "ficar" for location.

## Chapter 6: How Brazilians Actually Talk

- Objective: decode everyday spoken Brazilian Portuguese and give the reader a safe informal register.
- English-speaker difficulty addressed: reduced forms, "a gente", "cê", object "ele", initial proclisis and discourse markers, which do not appear in book grammar.
- Pillar(s): Pillar 6 (decoder of spoken Portuguese).
- Sources: [F64] [F65] [F66] [F67] [F77] [F78] [F79] [F80] [F88].
- Planned boxes: "If you speak Spanish" box with three traps relevant to spoken register plus the full-stop b, d, g drill ("cada", "lado", "fogo") [F62] [F36]. No UK/Australian English box in this chapter.
- Self-test: 10 questions with answer key [F94].
- Spaced review plan: D+1, D+3, D+8 and D+27, the author's suggestion, an adaptation of the interval idea in [F90], which measures a single gap between two study sessions.
- Planned content:
  - informal register taught as normal use: "a gente vai", "tô", "tá", "pra", "né", "cadê", "vamo", markers "aí", "então", "tipo", "sei lá", "olha" [F77]; initial proclisis ("Me empresta dez reais?") [F65] and object "ele" [F64].
  - "cê(s)" accounts for 77% of the occurrences of "cê", "ocê" and "você" and for 85% of preverbal occurrences in the corpus studied [F79]; "você(s)" answered for 78.1% of treatment pronouns in that corpus, with the qualitative observation that foreigners overuse "o senhor" [F78]; "né" as an interactional marker [F80].
  - the contrast hook: spoken "tipo" works like spoken English "like"; the single English "you" against "você", "o senhor", "tu" and "cê" is the author's contrastive suggestion.
  - weekly routine: short dialogue with transcription, rewrite into book Portuguese, then back into spoken Portuguese, using the open materials of UT Austin and COERLL [F88].

- Pillar(s): Pillar 7 (politeness and refusal).

- Planned boxes: "If you speak Spanish" box with three traps relevant to politeness plus the full-stop b, d, g drill ("cada", "lado", "fogo") [F62] [F36]. No UK/Australian English box in this chapter.

- Spaced review plan: D+1, D+3, D+8 and D+27, the author's suggestion, an adaptation of the interval idea in [F90], which measures a single gap between two study sessions.
- Planned content:
  - past future form as politeness [F82], with author's illustrations "Poderia me ajudar?" and "Gostaria de um café."
  - veiled refusal: "Vou ver e te aviso" and "Vou ver se dá" as refusals [F83]; the documented case of "Passe lá em casa mais tarde!", where the visitor reads the timing and nobody says anything [F84], presented as a case to read carefully and not as a universal rule.
  - the diminutive carries affection and confidence, and saying no is hard in Brazilian interaction [F81].
  - work setting: American confidence can be read as naivety or arrogance; the case collection from the Brazilians Working with Americans project [F87].
  - scenario scripts in three versions (direct, polite, Brazilian) and a discussion of which one would sound rude; the author's suggestion that conditional politeness transfers positively from "Could you" and "I would like", worded as a suggestion.

## Chapter 8: If You Already Speak Spanish

- Objective: reconfigure the Spanish base quickly instead of fossilizing early, focusing on the sounds and the words where Spanish misleads.
- English-speaker difficulty addressed: Spanish approximants in place of full stops, Spanish-style vowels in mid contrasts, and vocabulary that looks identical and is not.
- Pillar(s): Pillar 8 (track for readers who already speak Spanish).
- Sources: [F36] [F48] [F62] [F75] [F76] [F107] [F108].

No UK/Australian English box in this chapter.
- Self-test: 10 questions with answer key [F94].
- Spaced review plan: D+1, D+3, D+8 and D+27, the author's suggestion, an adaptation of the interval idea in [F90], which measures a single gap between two study sessions.
- Planned content:
  - bilingual English-Spanish speakers depend on Spanish when learning Portuguese whatever the acquisition order [F62]; initial transfer does not facilitate and requires reconfiguration [F76], and typological similarity weighs [F75].
  - the phonological risk: voiced stops between vowels produced as Spanish-style approximants [F36], and the risk of early fossilization [F108].
  - the upside: intensive courses for this profile report very fast progress [F107].
  - drill plan for full (stop) b, d, g in Portuguese, and the mid-vowel warning for Spanish-trained ears.

## Chapter 9: Your Error Deck: Reviewing the English Speaker's Typical Mistakes

- Objective: turn the book's traps into a small, self-tested review deck with an interleaving rule and a correction routine.
- English-speaker difficulty addressed: persistent errors that fossilize in adult interlanguage, and review habits based on rereading instead of recall.
- Pillar(s): Pillar 9 (the English speaker's error deck).
- Sources: [F50] [F71] [F73] [F90] [F94] [F97] [F102] [F103] [F104].
- Planned boxes: "If you speak Spanish" box with three traps relevant to error review plus the full-stop b, d, g drill ("cada", "lado", "fogo") [F62] [F36]. No UK/Australian English box in this chapter.
- Self-test: 10 questions with answer key [F94].
- Spaced review plan: D+1, D+3, D+8 and D+27, the author's suggestion, an adaptation of the interval idea in [F90], which measures a single gap between two study sessions.
- Planned content:
  - deck rules only, no memory theory: self-testing with feedback beats restudy [F94], spacing helps [F90], and interleaving helps in similar categories but hurts in plain word lists [F97], so interleaving is applied only to pairs the English speaker confuses.
  - what goes into the deck and nothing else: transferred present perfect [F71]; voice-onset-time and mid-vowel pairs [F24] [F18], with the limitation that [F24] studies the reverse direction (Brazilians learning English), so the voice-onset-time pairs are an inference for this audience; core false friends ("atualmente", "pretender", "parentes") [F43] [F39] [F46]; copied regência [F73].
  - correction routine: ask a tutor or partner to correct the same errors; corrective oral feedback has significant and lasting effects [F102], implicit correction lasts longer [F104], and learners tend to want more correction than teachers think they should give, as reported in the abstract of [F103].
  - persistent errors can fossilize in adult interlanguage [F50], which is why the deck is small and repeated.
